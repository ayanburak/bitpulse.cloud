## Headline research and decision

The research opened these pages:

- Google Search Central on title links: there is no fixed limit, but titles are cut to the device width, and Google asks for titles that are descriptive and concise.
- Google Search Central on Article structured data: long titles may be truncated on some devices.
- Google Search Central on Discover: avoid exaggerated or misleading titles and withheld key information.
- Nielsen Norman Group on BBC News headlines: they average about 34 characters, with key words first.
- Nielsen Norman Group on microcontent: page titles of 40 to 60 characters.
- journalism.co.uk on BBC dual headlines: 31 to 33 characters on the front page, up to 55 in search.
- Outbrain, 2014: on its paid links, headlines of 60 to 100 characters had the highest click-through. This is weak evidence.
- seocrawl.ai, a Turkish SEO vendor: 50 to 70 characters in Google results.
- A BirGün column on headlines: clarity comes first.

No Turkish newsroom guidance on length and no figure for chat app previews or RSS readers was found. The Guardian style guide and the X documentation could not be opened.

The decision is that headlines should change. Bitpulse news headlines ran from 77 to 107 characters, plus 11 for " | Bitpulse" in the page title, above every figure found. The proposed rule sets English at 50 to 65 characters, with 70 as the limit, and Turkish at 55 to 70, with 75 as the limit. A headline is one clause, with no two points joined by "and", and the key name comes first. The limits sit above the BBC's figures because the style guide asks headlines to name people and companies.

## The explainer

The question research could not open Google Trends, Reddit or Quora, so its evidence of demand rests on search result snapshots and on how weak the pages that answer each question are. Of its six questions, the RAM price question ranked first, but it dates quickly. The question on the energy and water used by AI prompts is close to the subject of the earlier AI explainer. The quantum encryption question was chosen because it lasts, has primary sources and had a fresh hook. NIST's final IR 8547 was published on 27 October 2026, and Google published estimates in March 2026.

The English body has about 1,730 words and the Turkish body about 1,380. The image is a CC BY 4.0 Wikimedia Commons photo by FMNLab of people assembling the cooling stack of a superconducting quantum computer.

The fact check opened all 13 sources and narrowed two claims. A claim about "today's best chips" became Willow's 105 qubits, and in Turkish "for years" became "decades", as the NIST source has it. The editor cut a zero-knowledge proof aside and fixed a line that called the word "all" the first word of a quote.

The final checker made five fixes:

- It changed the summary's claim that secrets "are already being stolen" to say they can be recorded and opened later, because no source reports such theft.
- It changed Apple from "added" to "announced" PQ3 in February 2024.
- It restored Google's reason that the threat is relevant today, which the editor had cut.
- It corrected a Cloudflare quote that the fact checker had passed.
- It restored the fact-checked closing line.

Its search for newer reports found an Alice & Bob preprint of 29 October 2026 estimating about 22,600 cat qubits for RSA-2048. That falls inside the range the story gives, so the story was not changed. No return to the writer was needed.
